\section{Project management approach}

The project ran in two connected phases (Chapter~\ref{ch:introduction}). The first was an AI and robustness phase, covering classification, subject-wise evaluation and the two robustness axes. The second was a hardware phase, covering quantisation and embedded and FPGA deployment. Work was tracked notebook by notebook in a version-controlled repository, with progress logs kept next to the code. That discipline produced the audit trail that later caught two real errors, a data-split bug (Section~\ref{sec:method-lowlight-data}) and a preprocessing mismatch (Section~\ref{sec:qr-runtime-correctness}). Supervisor meetings were used to check the direction of the project, most importantly about adding a multi-view stage and about continuing to try on-device inference after an early diagnosis suggested it might not be possible. Figure~\ref{fig:project-gantt} gives a more detailed view of the schedule. Report writing and the literature survey ran alongside the rest of the work rather than as separate steps, so they are shown as overlapping bars.

\section{Dataset preparation and splitting strategy}
\label{sec:method-splits}

\subsection{Single-view baseline: State Farm}

The State Farm Distracted Driver Detection dataset \citep{statefarm2016dataset} gives 22{,}424 labelled images across ten classes (safe driving, four texting or phone-use classes split by hand, adjusting the radio, drinking, reaching behind, hair or makeup, talking to a passenger), from a single side-mounted camera across 26 drivers. A simple random image-level split and a subject-wise split that holds entire drivers out were compared directly, and the subject-wise protocol was used throughout the rest of this project. A random split lets the same driver's appearance and background appear on both sides of the split, made worse by near-duplicate video frames landing on both sides too. This inflates the measured accuracy without actually showing that the model generalises to a driver it has not seen. Class balance across the resulting splits was checked, not just assumed (Figure~\ref{fig:sf-split-dist}).

\RequiredFigure[0.76\textwidth]{state_farm_subject_wise_split_class_distribution.png}{Class distribution across the State Farm subject-wise train/validation/test splits.}{fig:sf-split-dist}

\subsection{Multi-view extension: SAM-DD}

SAM-DD \citep{yang2024samdd} was recorded on an indoor driving simulator across 42 subjects. It was chosen because it gives paired front and side views of the same subjects doing comparable actions, a stronger requirement than just finding a second single-view dataset. This simulator origin is a real limitation, recorded in the metadata rather than glossed over.

SAM-DD has no official file mapping its numbered class folders to class names, so the mapping used here came from a direct visual check of sample images, with a confidence level (high, medium or low) recorded per class. Two SAM-DD classes, adjusting glasses and fatigue or head-drop, do not match anything in State Farm's taxonomy, and were left out completely rather than forced onto the nearest class, which would have quietly mislabelled the data.

A shared metadata schema was set up (\texttt{image\_path}, \texttt{label\_id}, \texttt{label\_name}, \texttt{subject\_id}, \texttt{dataset\_name}, \texttt{camera\_view}, \texttt{vehicle\_id}, \texttt{lighting}, \texttt{modality}, \texttt{split}, \texttt{is\_synthetic}, \texttt{notes}) so any dataset could be added without redesigning the pipeline. Left and right classes needed extra care once a second camera angle was added, since a front camera mirrors the image relative to a side-mounted one, so left and right were defined by the driver's own hand throughout, never by frame side.

\subsection{Lighting extension: the Low-Light Driver Distraction Dataset}
\label{sec:method-lowlight-data}

The lighting stage uses the Novel Driver Distraction Dataset with low-lighting support \citep{saad2020lowlight,saad2020dataset}, 70 driver folders across the same ten-class taxonomy, 52{,}350 images in total, captured through a NoIR-style camera under active infrared light. As explained in Section~\ref{sec:lit-lowlight}, this replaced an earlier plan to make synthetic infrared-like images from State Farm's daylight data. Preparing it produced three findings, all found by direct checking rather than taken from the dataset's own documentation.

\textbf{The dataset is a mix of day and night, not all night.} A per-driver brightness check (average greyscale brightness of one frame, cut-off at 40, confirmed by looking directly at over eighteen drivers) found only 9 of the 70 drivers are real night footage. The other 61 were recorded in daylight through the same infrared-capable camera. Taking the dataset's name at face value would have meant training and testing mostly on daytime images while calling the result night-time. The real night subset is 6{,}750 images, not 52{,}350.

\textbf{Four of the ten classes have an unresolved hand-side problem.} \texttt{texting\_left}\allowbreak /\allowbreak \texttt{texting\_right} and \texttt{phone\_left}\allowbreak /\allowbreak \texttt{phone\_right} are each a clear ``action'' pair (texting held low against phone at the ear), but the driver's left and right hands could not be told apart from this dataset's single side-profile angle in any sample checked, confirmed by two separate checks. These classes were kept mapped rather than dropped, matching what was done for SAM-DD, with a low-confidence note recorded that Section~\ref{sec:qr-class-errors} comes back to.

\textbf{A real split bug was found and fixed.} The first version pooled all 70 drivers together and, purely by chance of the random seed, placed zero of the 9 night drivers in the test split, so a ``night'' evaluation would have had no night data at all while still looking like it ran correctly. The fix was to split the night and day pools separately, each at 70/15/15. This is exactly the kind of error that gives a confident but wrong result instead of an obvious failure, and it was caught by an explicit check added right after the split.

For the lighting experiment, the merged table combines State Farm (day, RGB, side) with only the night subset on purpose. The 45{,}600 daytime low-light images were left out too, since putting them in the ``day'' bucket would bring in a sensor difference alongside the lighting difference, mixing two domain gaps into one number.

\section{Model selection and training}

Three architectures were compared on the State Farm baseline, a custom CNN as a from-scratch reference point, MobileNetV2 \citep{sandler2018mobilenetv2} and EfficientNet-B0 \citep{tan2019efficientnet}, the last two using transfer learning from ImageNet weights with a new head. All three used the same subject-wise split and were tested on accuracy, macro F1, weighted F1, per-class precision/recall, a confusion matrix, plus throughput and model size as deployment metrics distinct from accuracy. MobileNetV2 was carried forward because of its balance of accuracy and throughput and its comparatively small size.

Both robustness stages reuse the same two-stage protocol so their results can be compared directly. The backbone is first frozen with only the new head trained for 10 epochs, then the last 30 MobileNetV2 layers are unfrozen and fine-tuned at $1\times10^{-5}$, the recipe already proven in the single-view phase, for a further 10 epochs, with early stopping (patience 5) restoring the best validation epoch. Fine-tuning was not applied from the first epoch on purpose, since updating a large pretrained backbone before the head has learned anything useful risks destroying the pretrained features. Reporting the frozen result next to the fine-tuned one shows how big the underlying robustness problem actually is. Training used a fixed random seed on a single machine, which makes each run reproducible but does not show run-to-run variance (Section~\ref{sec:eval-limitations}).

\section{Quantisation and on-device deployment}

\subsection{Model conversion}
\label{sec:method-conversion}

The trained Keras model (HDF5, \texttt{keras\_version 2.10.0} in its own metadata) had to be converted to TensorFlow Lite for the ZC702's ARM core, in an isolated Python 3.9/TensorFlow 2.10.0 environment matching the model's exact save version. The main environment (TensorFlow 2.18 and Keras 3) could load the file through a compatibility shim, but the export it produced was one the board's runtime could not run at all.

A second bug showed up during conversion. The model's data-augmentation layer kept leaking unsupported operations into the exported graph, even with \texttt{training=False} set at trace time, because \texttt{Sequential.layers} is a read-only computed property in this Keras version, so clearing it silently does nothing, confirmed by printing the layer list before and after and finding it identical. The fix was to monkey-patch the layer's bound \texttt{.call} method to the identity function before tracing, checked two ways, zero occurrences of the leaked operation names in the exported binary, and outputs numerically identical to the original model. INT8 post-training quantisation was then applied using a 120-image representative dataset \citep{tensorflowlite}.

\subsection{Board bring-up and the ARM32 runtime issue}
\label{sec:arm32-issue}

The ZC702 (Zynq-7000, XC7Z020, dual-core Cortex-A9), loaned from the university electronics lab, was set up with the community PYNQ image \citep{pynq} (Ubuntu 18.04, Python 3.6), needed because the official Vitis-AI and DPU tooling does not support this chip family at all. Bring-up found and fixed two real defects in the image's own Jupyter service. But the bigger problem was that the only \texttt{tflite\_runtime} wheel ever published for this platform (2.5.0, the only one that has ever existed) loaded models correctly but crashed with \texttt{Illegal instruction} the moment \texttt{interpreter.invoke()} started computing. Memory exhaustion was ruled out, both quantisation schemes crashed the same way, and disabling XNNPACK changed nothing. Checking \texttt{/proc/cpuinfo} showed this Cortex-A9 supports \texttt{vfpv3} but not \texttt{vfpv4}, so it has no fused multiply-add instruction, while the published wheel targets \texttt{neon-vfpv4}, the profile common to Raspberry Pi 3 and 4 hardware. This is a real CPU instruction-set mismatch, not a configuration or model problem, documented on this SoC family.

The runtime was rebuilt from source directly on the board, which needed CMake to be bootstrapped from source first, because the board's operating system is older than any usable prebuilt CMake for this architecture. Building natively, instead of cross-compiling, let GCC's \texttt{-march=native} flag detect the actual CPU present, so the compiler never emits an instruction the chip lacks, a more accurate fix than just ``targeting VFPv3''. This fixed the crash, confirmed by measuring prediction agreement against the reference runtime (Section~\ref{sec:qr-runtime-correctness}).

\section{FPGA alert-controller design and verification}
\label{sec:fpga-alert-design}

A full CNN accelerator in fabric was assessed early on, in line with the IPR's own fallback plan, and judged unlikely to reach a demonstrable standard in the time available \citep{lu2025hardware,parra2023methodology}. Instead, the FPGA work targets the decision stage that comes after the classifier. \texttt{distraction\_alert\_controller.v} implements confidence-hysteresis logic in fixed-point Verilog. It raises an alert only after eight confident ``distracted'' ticks in a row, and clears it only after eight confident ``safe'' ticks in a row, using the same 40\% threshold as the software-side smoothing fix already proven on the live demo. This mirrors a software fix already proven in the project rather than a separate hardware rule, aiming for a design justified with evidence, not just specified on paper.

Two levels of verification were used. The first was a standalone self-checking testbench that runs the hysteresis logic against synthetic edge cases, ten checks in total. The second replayed 28 real predictions from the trained cross-view model on real test images, sent bit by bit over a simulated UART link through the receive-decode-decide-alert chain, including two real misclassifications, the input the hysteresis logic exists to absorb. The controller was then synthesised out-of-context for a Digilent Basys3 board (\texttt{xc7a35tcpg236-1}, Artix-7), a low-cost target chosen on purpose, using Vivado 2017.4 in batch mode.

\section{Alert delivery, tools and test strategy}

To close the loop from a classification to a notification a driver or fleet operator would actually receive, the board runs a continuous capture-classify loop whose hysteresis logic is a tick-for-tick Python copy of the Verilog controller. It posts each alert, with its triggering frame, to a hosted FastAPI backend that serves an Android client. A physical buzzer wired to the board gives a second, separate alert channel, fired on the same rising edge as the network post. Driving it needed a purpose-built PYNQ overlay, a minimal Vivado block design (Zynq processing system plus one AXI GPIO bit), synthesised, implemented and loaded onto the ZC702's \texttt{xc7z020} silicon. It exposes a pin that was previously not controllable at all, confirmed against the board's user guide \citep{xilinx2019ug850}, the one part of this project's FPGA work that was verified on real hardware rather than estimated (Section~\ref{sec:qr-fpga}). Full detail is in Appendix~\ref{app:buzzer}. The full path was checked in three layers, set out in Appendix~\ref{app:alertpath}. What was not done is an unattended physical demonstration of someone staying distracted long enough to trigger the alert on their own, and the layered verification here is the honest substitute for that.

The AI pipeline used Python, TensorFlow/Keras (2.10.1 for GPU training on an NVIDIA RTX 3060, 2.10.0 for conversion), OpenCV, NumPy and pandas in Jupyter notebooks with reusable \texttt{src/} modules, and hardware work used Vivado 2017.4 and the ZC702's own PYNQ Linux environment. Testing was applied at every stage, not only at the end. Dataset adapters were checked with explicit assertions, no unmapped labels, no row-count loss, no subject in more than one split, right after each dataset was folded into the unified table, so a data bug would show up before it could waste a training run, which is exactly what caught the night-driver split bug. Model evaluation used held-out subject-wise test sets throughout. The conversion pipeline was checked structurally (no leaked operations in the exported graph) and numerically (matching output against the original model), not just by confirming conversion finished, and on-device deployment was held to the same standard, measuring board-versus-reference agreement directly rather than treating ``the crash is gone'' as proof of correctness.

\section{Ethical, legal, professional and social issues}

Ethics approval was not needed. All data comes from existing public secondary datasets \citep{statefarm2016dataset,yang2024samdd,saad2020dataset}, with no participants recruited and no third-party data collected, and live qualitative testing used the researcher's own footage. No dataset is included in the repository, all stored locally and left out through \texttt{.gitignore}, matching each dataset's own terms. SAM-DD in particular is licensed strictly for non-commercial use, with no redistribution allowed.

None of the three datasets records demographic information such as age, ethnicity or body type, so no claim can be made that these figures generalise beyond these specific groups of subjects. A driver-monitoring system also raises a real privacy concern, pointing a camera at a person on every journey, though the design helps somewhat, since inference runs entirely on the board and only a single alert event leaves the device, not a continuous stream. But the alert-image feature does send the one triggering frame, a trade-off that should be user-configurable in any real product. In line with the BCS Code of Conduct \citep{bcs2026conduct}, this system is described throughout as a research prototype that shows technical feasibility, not a validated safety product. A missed detection and a false alert both carry real consequences in a vehicle, and the second is arguably worse, since a system that cries wolf too often gets ignored or turned off, exactly why the hysteresis stage exists.

In practice, the biggest constraint was getting hold of hardware, not modelling difficulty. The ZC702 was loaned partway through the project, which fixed the earliest possible start date for on-device work, and as a roughly \pounds1{,}200 asset that had to be returned in working condition, it ruled out any changes carrying real risk (Section~\ref{sec:eval-challenges}).
