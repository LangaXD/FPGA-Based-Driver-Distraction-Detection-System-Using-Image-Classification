\chapter{Literature Review}

\section{How the literature for this project was selected}
\label{sec:lit-prisma}

This chapter is not just a collection of papers gathered while writing. It is based on a systematic, PRISMA-style search and screening exercise, carried out as foundation work in June 2026 and written up separately as a survey paper. Reporting the selection process matters for the same reason PRISMA exists \citep{page2021prisma,prisma2020}, since a hidden search method cannot be judged for coverage or bias, and a reader cannot tell whether a missing topic was searched for and not found, or simply never looked for.

Searches ran across IEEE Xplore, Google Scholar, SpringerLink, MDPI, Elsevier and ScienceDirect sources, arXiv, dataset repositories and citation chasing, using three groups of search phrases matching this project's three strands, RGB distraction classification, detection and embedded deployment, and lighting robustness. The lighting group used terms such as ``night-time driver distraction detection'' and ``near infrared driver monitoring'', added after supervisor feedback found lighting robustness under-covered relative to its importance.

The selection process is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:prisma}. The search found 90 records, and after 22 duplicates were removed, 68 were screened by title and abstract, 29 of which were excluded for being out of scope, too general or technically incomplete. Thirty-nine full texts were then checked and eight were excluded, leaving 31 eligible papers, of which 20 were chosen as core studies and 11 were kept as supporting literature. A per-source breakdown is in Appendix~\ref{app:prisma}.

\RequiredFigure[0.76\textwidth]{fig_prisma_summary.png}{PRISMA-style selection process for the systematic search underpinning this review (90 records identified, 20 core studies selected).}{fig:prisma}

The papers cited below are drawn from that eligible set, plus a few later sources found during the project, and where one was already among the 20 core studies that is on purpose, not coincidence. Two things from the survey shape how this chapter reads. First, reported accuracy is hard to compare across papers (Figure~\ref{fig:reported-accuracy}), since a 99\% figure from a random image-level split and an 85\% figure from a subject-wise split are not measuring the same thing, and the higher number is often actually the weaker result. Second, deployment claims are far more common than deployment evidence (Figure~\ref{fig:deployment-maturity}). Most studies stop at reported accuracy, fewer report laptop throughput, and only a small number report measurements on the embedded target itself, which is exactly why Chapter~\ref{ch:results} makes a point of keeping laptop figures separate from on-device ones.

\section{Image-based driver distraction detection}

Image-based distraction detection is a well-established use of convolutional neural networks. \citet{valeriano2018recognition} trained a CNN directly on the State Farm dataset used here and reported strong within-view accuracy, showing that the ten-class taxonomy can be learned from a single side-mounted camera with no extra sensors. \citet{alshalfan2021detecting} and \citet{aljasim2022e2dr} build on this. The former compares several deep architectures on the same problem, and the latter proposes an ensemble that also recommends a response once distraction is detected, treating classification as one stage of a larger decision pipeline, close to the position taken here, where the decision stage after the classifier is its own designed part, not an afterthought.

\citet{abouelnaga2018} and \citet{eraqi2019driver} produced the AUC Distracted Driver dataset and an ensemble method built around it, the clearest proof that the State Farm taxonomy carries over to an independently collected dataset, which is why AUC V2 was the first dataset asked for when the multi-view stage began, although access was not given within the project timeline. Broader reviews by \citet{kashevnik2021driver}, \citet{horgan2021} and \citet{dontoh2025visual} confirm a pattern more than any single result, that reported systems are almost always tested at one camera position under one lighting condition, treated as fixed dataset properties rather than variables to test.

Two architectures come up again and again in this literature and were used directly here. MobileNetV2 \citep{sandler2018mobilenetv2} uses inverted residual blocks with linear bottlenecks to keep computation and parameter count low, which is why it is common in driver-monitoring work that expects embedded deployment, both here and in \citet{lu2025hardware}. EfficientNet-B0 \citep{tan2019efficientnet} instead scales depth, width and resolution together under a fixed compute budget, included here as a comparison point with a different design idea behind it.

\section{Subject-wise evaluation, dataset bias and domain shift}

A methodological problem that recurs across this field, and is treated seriously here, is subject leakage. If the same driver appears in both the training and test partitions, a random image-level split over-estimates real-world accuracy, since the model can learn driver-specific appearance cues, such as clothing, build, seat position and background, instead of the behaviour itself, and consecutive video frames make this even worse. This is the most common reason published figures in this field cannot be compared with each other, and it is why the subject-wise protocol in Section~\ref{sec:method-splits} is used the same way across every dataset and stage in this project. \citet{wang2023driver} look at a related but different problem, domain shift across recording conditions, and report a measurable drop when a model trained under one set of conditions is tested under another. This points the same way as both robustness findings in this project, although the size of the drop measured here for a real lighting shift (Section~\ref{sec:qr-lowlight}) is far more severe.

\section{Multi-view and cross-view driver monitoring}
\label{sec:lit-crossview}

The fact that most systems are checked on only one camera view is not because multi-view data or methods do not exist. \citet{jegham2019mdad} introduced MDAD, a multi-view, multi-modal driver action dataset from synchronised side and front cameras, similar to SAM-DD \citep{yang2024samdd} as used here, confirming synchronised-view pairing is an established way to build cross-view data, not invented for this project. \citet{ortega2020dmd} and \citet{wang2023hundreddriver} take this further to larger multi-modal collections. \citet{sengar2023poseviNet} take a different approach, pulling pose information per view rather than pooling raw images and combining it with a vision transformer, suggesting viewpoint-aware features are a real alternative to the fine-tuning approach used here.

The most directly relevant work is \citet{bhalla2025crossview}, which frames cross-view monitoring as a two-stage problem, first learning viewpoint-invariant features through contrastive learning across views, then adapting them across sensor types without labelled target data, and reports a large improvement over earlier baselines. That is a much more advanced approach than the one used here, which fine-tunes the last 30 layers of an already-trained backbone on pooled multi-view data. The comparison is useful, since the simpler approach here still recovered most of the accuracy lost when the frozen-backbone model was tested across views (Section~\ref{sec:qr-crossview}), suggesting well-done fine-tuning on real paired data is a reasonable middle ground for a project of this size, even though contrastive adaptation is still the more principled direction.

Where this project is different from all of the above is that it measures the frozen-backbone cross-view gap on purpose ``before'' applying any fix. Few of the reviewed papers report an equivalent ``before'' baseline, which makes it hard to judge how large the raw gap usually is for a MobileNetV2-class model, or how much of each paper's improvement actually comes from its method rather than from simply having multi-view training data at all.

\section{Low-light, infrared and near-infrared driver monitoring}
\label{sec:lit-lowlight}

Driver monitoring at night is probably the condition where a system like this matters most, and it is clearly under-covered in the RGB-classification literature. The most directly relevant work is \citet{saad2020lowlight}, who built the Novel Driver Distraction Dataset with low-lighting support \citep{saad2020dataset}, exactly because existing benchmarks were daylight-only, and this is the dataset used in this project (Section~\ref{sec:method-lowlight-data}). \citet{janveja2020insight} treat low-light driver-state monitoring as an embedded systems problem, not just a modelling one.

The most important paper here, in terms of method, is \citet{shen2023visible}, who cover daytime and night-time detection using unpaired visible-to-infrared image translation, generating infrared-like images from visible ones. This shows both the strength and the limit of the synthetic approach at once, a real, well-done way to add to scarce night training data, but the paper is clear that translated images only approximate real infrared capture, not replace it. That distinction matters a lot here. The original plan for this project's lighting stage was exactly this kind of transformation, dropped on purpose once real low-light data became available, since a synthetic transform cannot reproduce what an infrared light actually does to a scene, the loss of colour information, the way skin and fabric reflect near-infrared light and the sensor noise of a camera working at its limit. Because of this, Section~\ref{sec:qr-lowlight} uses real footage captured under active infrared light, and every conclusion about lighting robustness in this report rests on that real footage, not on an approximation.

\section{Quantisation and embedded/FPGA deployment}
\label{sec:lit-embedded}

The second half of this project is backed by a strand of literature that cares less about accuracy and more about what a model costs to run once it leaves a workstation. \citet{abushahla2025quantization} survey quantisation from a hardware point of view, covering both the techniques and the ARM and RISC-V platforms they target, and confirm that INT8 post-training quantisation, the approach used here, is the standard starting point for edge deployment, usually at a cost of a few percentage points, matching the 2.7 and 4.6-point costs measured on this project's own models (Section~\ref{sec:qr-quantisation}). \citet{hariharan2023edge} report a similar exercise for driver monitoring on edge AI hardware.

Two papers cover the FPGA side directly. \citet{parra2023methodology} present an open-source method for running a TensorFlow-Lite-quantised CNN through a custom AXI-Lite accelerator on a Zynq board (a Digilent Zybo-Z7, the same SoC family as the ZC702 used here), dealing with the fact that TensorFlow Lite has no built-in way of running on FPGA fabric. \citet{lu2025hardware} build a dedicated hardware and software accelerator specifically for MobileNetV2-based driver behaviour recognition on a Zynq XC7Z020, the exact chip used on the ZC702, reporting 13.05 FPS at 1.25 W.

Both of these are far more ambitious than the path taken here, which runs the classifier on the Cortex-A9 core using a runtime built from source and only uses the fabric for the decision logic, a choice about scope, not something overlooked. \citet{lu2025hardware}'s own figures back this up, since their design and verification effort took multiple weeks even with an existing architecture to build on. The cost of the choice made here shows up as speed, roughly 9.15 seconds per inference against those FPS-range figures, reported honestly here as a real limitation, not hidden.

\section{Gaps identified and positioning of this project}

Figure~\ref{fig:landscape} places this project against the surveyed landscape. Figure~\ref{fig:dataset-method} shows which method families have been applied to which datasets. The empty regions here are just as informative as the full ones. Four gaps come out of this, and each one maps onto a research question from Section~\ref{sec:research-questions}.

\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=0.2em]
	\item \textbf{Camera-view robustness is claimed more often than it is measured.} Few papers report the size of the problem for a simple baseline before fixing it (RQ2).
	\item \textbf{Lighting robustness is usually approximated rather than tested on real infrared-illuminated data} \citep{shen2023visible}. This project measures the day-to-night collapse on real captured footage (RQ3).
	\item \textbf{Quantisation and deployment costs are often reported partially or not at all.} Laptop throughput is often given with no matching on-target measurement (RQ4).
	\item \textbf{The decision stage that turns per-frame predictions into a stable alert is rarely treated as its own designed and tested part of the system} (RQ5).
\end{enumerate}
